Halo should preserve Ring's picture and sound as far as the selected HomeKit stream permits. The receiver-first audit found concrete compatibility and timing defects, plus local quality policies that need reconsideration. It does not yet establish one cause for every stutter.

This report covers the October 28 investigation, including the October 29 UTC capture. Research after the change of direction used existing metadata, source inspection and synthetic probes. No camera recording or new server deployment was performed.

The installed server is R4d, commit `9c51a33`. R5, commit `4e0af7b`, is tested and committed locally but not deployed. The R6a changes that preserve original frame timing and worker ownership are frozen in the working checkout; the new clock implementation remains an isolated candidate. These states matter when attributing a symptom to code.

The historical Apple-authored HAP R2 specification defines selected bitrate as codec output averaged over one second, and frame rate as a maximum. It requires SPS/PPS aggregation, media sampling timestamps and a shared audio/video wall-clock reference. It does not establish Halo's 500 ms deadline as an Apple playback requirement. The historical five-second keyframe wording needs qualification before changing recovery behavior. [Apple HAP R2, tables 9-10/9-28 and sections 11.2/11.8, public mirror](https://forum.iobroker.net/assets/uploads/files/1634848447889-apple-spezifikation-homekit.pdf).

Apple's current public developer-preview guide describes a newer HEVC/multitier camera protocol, with a 48 kHz Opus transmission clock. Halo is using the classic selected-stream protocol. Mixing the new guide's tiers or clock with the existing session would be a protocol change, not a correction to this stream. [Apple's 2026 compatibility guide](https://developer.apple.com/download/files/HomeKit-Secure-Video-Open-Source-Compatibility-Guide.pdf).

The audit distinguishes what Home asked for, what Halo currently does, and what remains unproven:

| Area | Evidence | Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Session selection | Home initially requested 1280×720, maximum 30 fps, High/4.0, 299 kbps. It requested 640×360 and 132 kbps once, approximately six seconds into the latest session. | There is an early quality reduction. Its trigger remains unknown. The recurring two-second disturbance is not repeated Home reconfiguration. |
| H.264 parameter packets | A synthetic probe of the real packetizer produced separate NAL types 7, 8 and 5. No aggregate packet was produced. | Concrete Home-facing packetization gap. Generic decoder tests accepted this arrangement and therefore missed the receiver-specific requirement. |
| Video clock | Reproductions show a two-second same-source reconfiguration gap compressed into one 33 ms timestamp step, and presentation jumps when source sender reports arrive later. | Proven clock defects; their contribution to the physical symptoms has not yet been isolated. |
| Audio clock | A 60 ms input can yield three 20 ms outputs assigned the same fallback presentation time. | Preserve each output's sample time rather than scheduling all three from the same arrival time. |
| Bit allocation | The encoder assumes nominal 30 fps while the observed source is about 15 fps. Existing native comparisons retained the same frames but used substantially less bitrate with current constant-rate accounting. | Precise frame timestamps alone do not ensure correct encoder bit allocation. Qualify source-time rate control before adopting it. |
| Sending policy | Halo uses selected codec kbps as a strict rate for protected network packets and applies a 500 ms original-receipt deadline. | Codec compliance, transport capacity and chosen latency have been conflated. They need separate measurements and bounds. |
| Additional R5 limits | Undeployed R5 reduces the codec target to 86% and VBV to 250 ms. Installed R4d uses the full selected target and a one-second VBV. | R5's added restrictions cannot explain the already observed R4d stutter. Their quality cost must be reviewed before deployment. |
| Incoming continuity | The last capture contained 81 joint audio/video quiet periods of at least 500 ms at the incoming UDP callback, typically 700 ms long and two seconds apart. | A Home-facing repair alone may not remove every pause. The cause of that incoming cadence remains unresolved. |
| Receiver feedback | Home reported little loss, one picture-recovery request and no retransmission requests. Reported jitter was high. | Investigate timing, but do not equate reported jitter with playback latency or a proven network fault. |

The [pinned Scrypted packetizer](https://github.com/koush/scrypted/blob/50b8668ea8d36f281d5ab6d152cd47f3d45e57f5/plugins/homekit/src/types/camera/h264-packetizer.ts#L355-L375) builds an SPS/PPS aggregate before an IDR; its nearby comments explicitly identify separate parameter packets as incompatible with HomeKit. Its ordinary video path often preserves encoded video, and its optional encoder uses a different buffering policy. These are useful implementation comparisons, not proof that every Ring stream can bypass conversion.

The [pinned Homebridge Ring sender](https://github.com/dgreif/ring/blob/516e96a24ec279168c246795e623b6bfdf58ec45/packages/homebridge-ring/camera-source.ts#L221-L254) also demonstrates encoded-video forwarding. Halo still needs to establish compatibility with the actual requested profile, level, dimensions and bitrate. Changing a codec label cannot make incompatible compressed content conform.

[RFC 3550](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3550.html#section-6.4.1) pairs RTP and wall-clock values at the same instant in sender reports; receiver jitter compares packet arrival spacing with media timestamp spacing. Consequently, correcting sender reports is necessary for synchronization but is not, by itself, an explanation for high reported jitter. A bounded local send-timing measurement should separate Halo's contribution from what Home reports.

I recommend the following revised repair sequence:

1. **Make the output verifiably match the negotiated contract.** Add the missing SPS/PPS aggregation and test actual protected packets, fragmentation, final-picture markers and recovery ordering. Report parsed output format separately from requested configuration; the existing `video_format` diagnostic currently describes the request.
2. **Correct the media clock.** Keep original frame ownership through conversion; maintain stable audio/video time across delayed output, source reports and reconfiguration. Preserve real source gaps. Retain the classic selected audio clock, whose behavior is also present in the reference implementations. Do not add filters, gain changes, artificial silence or time stretching.
3. **Recover avoidable picture-quality losses.** Separate encoded payload measurements from packet overhead and repair traffic. Compare encoder rate-control choices against actual source time, then qualify VBV and ordinary keyframe policy. Preserve encoded video where compatible; otherwise perform only the conversion required by the selected stream. Test color range, color metadata, aspect ratio and black levels as well as detail.
4. **Fit transport behavior to that stream.** Compare bounded burst handling and latency policies using the captured cadence. Keep finite queue, byte, age and repair limits. A larger allowance is an explicit latency tradeoff, not permission for unlimited buffering. Complete the separate retransmission and upstream keyframe-recovery work. If incoming gaps persist, investigate that connection separately rather than continually reducing output quality.
5. **Qualify on Apple Home.** Run controlled closed/open/sustained/closed comparisons, then reconnect, reconfigure and remote-view cases. Compare the same scene in Ring and Home with the owner's feedback. Pairing and HKSV-off settings remain preserved.

For synthetic qualification, measure encoded one-second windows, actual frame cadence, keyframe size, bounded queue age, audio duration and timestamp continuity. Independently decode moving-detail, text, dark/noisy and color-chart fixtures; use objective image comparisons alongside visual inspection. A successful decoder or a passing unit suite is insufficient evidence of crisp, uninterrupted Home playback.

For the real-camera comparison, collect only parsed format, timing and feedback metadata. Acceptance requires no recurring picture/sound interruptions, no repeating pixelation associated with recovery, stable synchronization, and a clear account of any discarded content. We also need to learn why Home reduces its requested quality. A smaller selected picture or materially lower codec budget limits attainable parity; Halo cannot recreate detail absent from its incoming stream.

Apple's internal adaptation and playback-buffer algorithms remain opaque. We can verify the protocol, reproduce Halo's timing defects, and measure Home's responses. We cannot honestly claim to know every internal receiver threshold or promise that these changes alone eliminate the incoming gaps.
